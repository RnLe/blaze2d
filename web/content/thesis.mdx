# Photonic moiré crystals

<p className="thesis-lead">How a two-scale description of twisted crystals led to Blaze2D.</p>

A photonic crystal controls light through a repeating pattern of dielectric material.
Twist two such patterns against each other and their local alignment changes slowly
across space. That extra structure can reshape the optical bands, reduce propagation
speeds and confine modes. The question behind this thesis was how to calculate those
effects directly from Maxwell’s equations without resolving every microscopic cell
of a large twisted structure.

The answer developed in *Photonic Band Theory of Moire Crystals: A Two-Scale Approach*
is an envelope theory built from local Bloch modes. It replaces one large calculation
with many small crystal calculations and a separate problem for the slow modulation.
Blaze2D was built to supply those local calculations, including the operator data
needed to connect them.

## Why direct calculations become expensive

A periodic band solver needs a repeating cell. A generic twist has no exact repeating
bilayer cell. Special, commensurate angles do, but those cells grow rapidly as the angle
shrinks. At a fixed microscopic resolution, the number of grid points grows roughly
as $\theta^{-2}$. Memory, factorization and repeated eigensolves then become the bottleneck.

This was concrete in the revised square-bilayer study. The FDFD reference at $2.01^\circ$
used about **3.3 million unknowns** and took **3,221 seconds**. The next cell at $1.01^\circ$
would need about 13 million unknowns and did not fit in the workstation’s memory.
An angle sweep therefore needed a description whose grid follows the slow physics.

## Two scales from one twist

Each monolayer has a lattice spacing $a$. Their changing alignment introduces the much
larger moiré period $L$. For identical lattices near alignment,

$$
L = \frac{a}{2\sin(\theta/2)},
\qquad
\eta = \frac{a}{L} = 2\sin(\theta/2) \simeq \theta.
$$

Here the small-angle approximation uses radians. A $2^\circ$ twist gives $L\approx29a$.

stacking pattern. Change the angle to see the two scales separate. The enlarged
monolayer cells keep their size while the moiré cell changes.

The moiré cell describes a repeating **pattern of local alignment**. It is not generally
an exact supercell of all the dielectric objects. That distinction is what lets the
theory address angles at which a periodic full-structure calculation is unavailable.
At large twists the geometric construction still works, but the two scales are no
longer well separated and the small-angle theory loses its basis.

## From Maxwell to a slow envelope

For a lossless, non-magnetic medium with scalar permittivity $\varepsilon(x,y)$, the
strictly two-dimensional problem separates into two polarizations:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{TE}:\quad -\nabla\!\cdot\!\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla H_z\right)
&= \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}H_z,\\[4pt]
\mathrm{TM}:\quad -\nabla^2 E_z
&= \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\varepsilon E_z.
\end{aligned}
$$

Locally, a small-angle bilayer resembles an ordinary periodic crystal with a relative
shift between its two constituents. This shift is the **registry**, $\boldsymbol\delta$.
Moving through the moiré pattern changes the registry slowly, so the local Bloch modes
can serve as a basis for the full field:

$$
\psi^\eta(\mathbf x)\approx
 e^{i\mathbf k_0\cdot\mathbf r}
 \sum_{n\in P}u_n\!\left(\mathbf r;\boldsymbol\delta(\mathbf R)\right)f_n(\mathbf R),
\qquad \mathbf r=\frac{\mathbf x}{a},\quad \mathbf R=\frac{\mathbf x}{L}.
$$

The $u_n$ resolve the fast crystal oscillations near a chosen carrier momentum
$\mathbf k_0$. The amplitudes $f_n$ describe the slow envelope. Only a selected group
of bands, $P$, is retained. Its separation from the other bands matters: a small twist
alone cannot make a poorly isolated band group into a valid reduced model.

## What the effective Hamiltonian contains

Projecting Maxwell’s operator onto these local modes and expanding through second
order in $\eta$ gives the following compact TE form:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal H_{\mathrm{eff}} ={}& \Lambda
+ \eta\left[\frac12\left(v^{(i)}\Pi_i+\Pi_i v^{(i)}\right)+U_{\mathrm{rem}}^{(1)}\right]\\
&+\eta^2\left[\frac12\Pi_i M^{-1}_{ij}\Pi_j+U_{\mathrm{rem}}^{(2)}\right]
+\mathcal O(\eta^3).
\end{aligned}
$$

It acts on the envelope via $\mathcal H_{\mathrm{eff}}f=\lambda f$, with the dimensionless
$\lambda=(\omega a/c)^2$. Repeated spatial indices are summed.

- **Local band energies** $\Lambda$ describe how the spectrum changes with registry.
- **Velocity and inverse mass** describe the local band slopes and curvatures. The mass includes virtual coupling to bands outside $P$ through Löwdin downfolding.
- **Covariant momentum** $\Pi_i=-i\mathcal D_i$ includes the Berry connection, which tracks how the retained Bloch basis changes from one registry to another.
- **Remainder operators** contain the remaining scalar and derivative terms, including the Born-Huang contribution from the basis changing into the discarded band space.

These coefficients are tabulated on the registry domain for a fixed material and band
choice, then reused at different twists. For TM, transforming the field with
$\widetilde E_z=\varepsilon^{1/2}E_z$ makes the eigenproblem Hermitian in the ordinary
inner product. Derivatives of the factor $\rho=\varepsilon^{-1/2}$ then contribute
additional terms. The complete expressions and definitions are collected in these
two annotated posters.

## Why this required Blaze2D

The reduced problem is smaller, but assembling it needs more than band frequencies.
Each registry point requires Bloch eigenvectors, velocity and mass matrix elements,
momentum and registry derivatives, and couplings between retained and remote bands.
Keeping four bands can require solving sixteen or more, repeated at thousands of
slightly different crystal configurations.

MPB provided a valuable reference. Its [Python interface](https://mpb.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Python_User_Interface/)
also exposes fields, raw eigenvectors and group velocities. The missing part for this
thesis was direct access to the specific projected operators and interband couplings
needed by the envelope construction. Those quantities were not available as the
operator dataset the pipeline required. Extracting them, with consistent normalization
and gauge conventions across a registry sweep, was a separate problem from computing
a band diagram.

Blaze2D combines that access with a Rust plane-wave solver designed for repeated local
solves. Iterative eigensolves, warm starts, mixed precision and parallel parameter
studies address throughput; explicit Bloch and operator outputs address the data
bottleneck. Blaze supplies the microscopic inputs. The **MSL research pipeline**
assembles and solves the moiré envelope problem from them.

## What the second edition establishes

The central test is whether the small envelope problem reproduces the full crystal’s
frequencies. **EA** labels the envelope approximation, assembled by MSL from Blaze’s
local Bloch data. The independent references solve Maxwell’s equations on the full
structure: **PWE** uses plane waves; **FDFD** uses a spatial grid. MPB adds a check
for the square bilayer. Every comparison uses the same dielectric and declared band domain.

Eigenvalues determine the crystal’s allowed frequencies. Similar-looking bands can
hide missing, extra or misplaced modes. Matching **every state in a range chosen
in advance** tests both the frequencies and their count. Agreement near the reference’s
own numerical error shows how much the small model preserves. Frequency differences
below use $f=\omega a/(2\pi c)$.

### Convergence under controlled conditions

A **frame** is the set of local Bloch field patterns used to build the envelope
solution. Recall how the blue and orange dots change their local alignment across
the explorer’s white cell. That change in registry also changes the local modes.

- A **frozen frame** reuses the modes from one registry and carrier momentum everywhere. The envelope can vary, but its microscopic field patterns stay fixed.
- A **registry-adapted frame** recalculates those patterns as the local alignment changes. The envelope is built on modes that follow the crystal underneath it.

The first plot asks whether a smaller twist makes the prediction more accurate.
On this smooth, weakly coupled crystal, adaptation lowers the error about fifteenfold.
At fixed material, however, both models reach an error floor: the registry potential
becomes stronger relative to the slow kinetic energy as the angle shrinks.

A controlled test reduces the second-layer amplitude as $a_2\propto\eta^2$, keeping
that balance fixed. Its adapted model converges approximately as **$\eta^{3.8}$**
and reaches agreement with both full-Maxwell references of about
**$8\times10^{-7}$ in frequency**. This rate belongs to that scaled family.

### Matching every state in a declared domain

A frame also depends on **momentum**, which sets the wave’s direction and wavelength.
The “exact frames” below use a Bloch solution at each retained momentum, summing beyond
the low-order expansion. This separate weak-coupling test uses modes of the
registry-averaged crystal.

At **$0.607^\circ$**, a supercell contains **8,911 microscopic cells**. The chosen domain
covers states near one minimum of the local band, called a valley. The exact-frame
model matches all **51** states in that domain, with frequency differences of
$4.7\times10^{-8}$ to $8.1\times10^{-8}$. These are comparable to the measured
numerical error of the full-Maxwell reference.

<ImageViewer src="/figures/thesis/second-edition/ladder_big.svg" title="All 51 states of the declared domain" alt="Spectral ladders and per-state errors comparing FDFD, fixed Bloch frames and exact momentum-dependent frames for all 51 in-domain states." caption="Figure 47. Each rung on the left is one allowed frequency; connecting lines pair the same state across models. The right panel resolves differences too small to see in the ladders. Exact frames recover all 51 states. The fixed-frame model has a smaller valid range and fails the full state count." />

Bands outside the retained set also influence how its modes propagate. The second-order
**Löwdin correction** includes this influence and recovers two to three orders of
accuracy on the affected states. **Feshbach downfolding** treats the same coupling
without that second-order truncation. Exact momentum-dependent frames improve it
further. The discarded bands contribute corrections, while the envelope state set
stays fixed.

### What remains unresolved at strong coupling

The square rod bilayer is harder: its changing registry creates deep wells that
confine the envelopes. The revised model brings the lowest frequency close to the
full-wave references and reaches **$0.50^\circ$**, where the full calculation did not
fit in memory. Higher in the spectrum, however, symmetry should make groups of four
modes share a frequency. The model splits these groups incorrectly. Its adapted
basis still needs a consistent phase and mixing convention, or **gauge**, that
respects the crystal’s symmetry.

### Does a smaller twist make flatter bands?

Flat bands and magic angles are of particular interest in physics. A study of band
flattening was therefore a natural application of the thesis’s effective model.

A moiré band traces an envelope mode’s frequency as its momentum changes. Its slope
sets the wave packet’s propagation speed, so flat bands are candidates for slow light.
The **bandwidth** measures the frequency spread along the sampled momentum path.

The pipeline scanned **237 angles** from $0.2^\circ$ to $10^\circ$. Above $1.5^\circ$,
the typical bandwidth follows roughly $\theta^{1.6}$. At smaller angles the fixed-grid
calculation plateaus. **No magic angle was established**: the shallow minimum does
not demonstrate a collapse to a flat band. Resolving that question would require
finer, angle-dependent grids and a direct check of the propagation velocity.

<ImageViewer src="/figures/thesis/second-edition/bandwidth_vs_angle.svg" title="Bandwidth across the twist-angle scan" alt="Per-mode and RMS miniband widths across 237 twist angles, including the theta to the power 1.6 fit and the low-angle plateau." caption="Figure 53. Top: bandwidths of six individual modes and the RMS average over twenty. Bottom: logarithmic axes expose the scaling trend and compare it with a quadratic law. The low-angle plateau is resolution-sensitive; the minimum near 1.05 degrees is not a demonstrated magic angle." />

Blaze exposes the local modes and operator matrix elements so a smaller model can
be assembled, tested and understood term by term. The second edition establishes that approach in controlled
regimes. Strong-coupling symmetry and an extension to photonic slabs remain open.
