# Preconditioner

<p className="pc-lead">Why Blaze never iterates with the raw residual, and how a cheap, deliberately inexact inverse of the Maxwell operator makes every eigensolver step count.</p>

Every band diagram Blaze computes is a sequence of eigenvalue problems: for each
wavevector $\mathbf{k}$, the lowest few eigenpairs of a discretized Maxwell operator.
The operator is far too large to diagonalize directly, so Blaze improves approximate
eigenvectors step by step with an iterative eigensolver. How many steps that takes
depends on one ingredient applied in every iteration: the **preconditioner**.

<PreconditionerQuestions />

## Why does Blaze need a preconditioner?

Blaze solves the eigenproblems with LOBPCG, the locally optimal block preconditioned
conjugate gradient method.<Citation id={1} /> It keeps a block $X$ of approximate
eigenvectors, one column per band, and improves the whole block at once:

</div>

<LobpcgCycle />

<div className="narrow">

The residuals measure what is still wrong, and Rayleigh–Ritz picks the best
combination of the directions on offer. **LOBPCG builds its trial subspace from the
preconditioned residual**: the only new information in an iteration is
$W = TR$, the residuals multiplied by the preconditioner $T$. If $T$ proposes good
corrections, a handful of iterations suffice. If it does not, Rayleigh–Ritz can only
choose among poor directions, and the iteration count grows with the resolution of
the grid.

The rest of this page builds up what $T$ is, starting from two equations.

## Which two equations matter?

</div>

<div className="pc-equations">
<div className="pc-equation">
<span className="pc-kicker">Linear system</span>

$$
A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}
$$

Given $A$ and $\mathbf{b}$, find the vector $\mathbf{x}$ that $A$ maps onto $\mathbf{b}$.

</div>
<div className="pc-equation" data-tone="eigen">
<span className="pc-kicker">Eigenvalue problem</span>

$$
A\mathbf{x} = \lambda\mathbf{x}
$$

Find the directions $\mathbf{x}$ that $A$ does not turn, only stretches by a factor $\lambda$.

</div>
</div>

<div className="narrow">

For a linear system, a preconditioner is a matrix $T \approx A^{-1}$ that is cheap to
apply. Iterative solvers then work with $TA\mathbf{x} = T\mathbf{b}$ instead of
$A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}$: the solution is the same, but $TA$ is close to the
identity, and for the identity there is nothing left to solve.<Citation id={3} /> With
$T = A^{-1}$ exactly, $\mathbf{x} = T\mathbf{b}$ in a single step. An eigenproblem has
no right-hand side, so $T$ enters through the residual, the eigenproblem's counterpart
of $\mathbf{b} - A\mathbf{x}$, as the next sections show.

Band calculations are eigenvalue problems. For Maxwell's equations they take the
generalized form $A\mathbf{x} = \lambda B\mathbf{x}$, where $\lambda$ is the squared
frequency and $B$ is the identity for TE and the permittivity for TM. This page uses
$B = I$, which keeps the geometry visible; nothing essential changes otherwise.

## What does a matrix do to a vector?

A matrix turns and stretches vectors. The playground uses a symmetric $2 \times 2$
matrix (Blaze's Maxwell operator is Hermitian, the complex counterpart), so its
eigenvalues are real and its eigenvectors perpendicular. Drag the tip of $\mathbf{x}$: the blue arrow is
<span className="pc-mark-image">$A\mathbf{x}$</span>. The faint blue ellipse is where
$A$ sends every vector of the same length as $\mathbf{x}$.

</div>

<VectorPlayground stage="operator" />

<div className="narrow">

Almost every direction is turned. Only along the two dashed lines does $A\mathbf{x}$
stay parallel to $\mathbf{x}$. Those are the eigenvectors $\mathbf{v}_1$ and
$\mathbf{v}_2$, and the stretch factors are the eigenvalues:

$$
A\mathbf{v}_i = \lambda_i \mathbf{v}_i, \qquad \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2 .
$$

Their ratio $\kappa = \lambda_2/\lambda_1$ is the **condition number**; the stretch
slider sets it. Blaze writes fields as sums of plane waves with wavevectors
$\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}$, where $\mathbf{G}$ runs over the reciprocal lattice
([Introduction](/introduction#how-does-blaze-solve-the-equation)). The eigenvalues of
the discretized Maxwell operator run from the lowest band up to the largest
$|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2$ the grid can represent, so $\kappa$ grows with the square
of the grid resolution. That growth is what a preconditioner has to undo.

## When is a vector almost an eigenvector?

For an exact eigenpair,

$$
A\mathbf{x} - \lambda\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0} .
$$

An iterative solver never holds an exact eigenvector, only an approximation. For a
given $\mathbf{x}$, the best eigenvalue estimate is the **Rayleigh quotient**

$$
\rho(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T} A\mathbf{x}}{\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{x}},
$$

the number $\mu$ that makes $\lVert A\mathbf{x} - \mu\mathbf{x}\rVert$ smallest. What
remains is the **residual**

$$
\mathbf{r} = A\mathbf{x} - \rho(\mathbf{x})\,\mathbf{x},
$$

which vanishes exactly when $\mathbf{x}$ is an eigenvector. Geometrically,
$A\mathbf{x}$ splits into a part along $\mathbf{x}$, namely $\rho\mathbf{x}$, and an
<span className="pc-mark-residual">orange remainder $\mathbf{r}$</span> perpendicular
to it: $\mathbf{x}^{\mathsf T}\mathbf{r} = 0$.

</div>

<VectorPlayground stage="residual" />

<div className="narrow">

Drag $\mathbf{x}$ onto a dashed line and the residual drops to zero. Its length
$\lVert\mathbf{r}\rVert$ is the natural measure of quality: a band is accurate once its
residual is small compared with $\lVert A\mathbf{x}\rVert$.

The residual also says how wrong $\mathbf{x}$ is, direction by direction. Expand
$\mathbf{x} = \sum_i c_i \mathbf{v}_i$ in the eigenvectors; then

$$
\mathbf{r} = \sum_i c_i\,(\lambda_i - \rho)\,\mathbf{v}_i .
$$

To find the lowest band, a correction must remove the components $c_i$ with $i > 1$.
The residual contains all of them, but each arrives multiplied by $\lambda_i - \rho$.

## What does the preconditioner change?

For a large operator, those factors are wildly uneven. Components with large
$\lambda_i$, the fine-scale ripples a grid resolves, dominate $\mathbf{r}$ even when
they are a negligible part of the error, while the components that matter for the
lowest bands barely register. A step along $\mathbf{r}$ spends almost all of its
effort on the stiffest directions.

Multiplying by an approximate inverse rebalances the factors. With $T = A^{-1}$,

$$
T\mathbf{r} = A^{-1}\mathbf{r} = \sum_i c_i\left(1 - \frac{\rho}{\lambda_i}\right)\mathbf{v}_i .
$$

Every factor is now at most $1$. Near convergence, $\rho \approx \lambda_1$: the wanted
component gets $1 - \rho/\lambda_1$, close to zero and slightly negative because
$\rho \ge \lambda_1$, while every unwanted component keeps a weight close to $1$. The <span className="pc-mark-preconditioned">preconditioned residual</span>
is essentially the error itself, and subtracting it moves $\mathbf{x}$ towards
$\mathbf{v}_1$. The bars show both sets of factors for the distinct eigenvalues
$\lambda_i \propto (2i-1)^2$ of a uniform one-dimensional crystal at the edge of the
Brillouin zone, with $\rho$ 8% above $\lambda_1$:

</div>

<div className="narrow">

The third playground adds the preconditioned residual. The teal arrow $-T\mathbf{r}$
starts at the tip of $\mathbf{x}$ and ends at the next iterate, marked by a dashed
ring: **Step** applies the plain preconditioned update
$\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} - T\mathbf{r}$, also called preconditioned inverse
iteration.<Citation id={4} /> The faint teal ellipse is $TA$ applied to the circle: the
rounder it is, the closer $TA$ is to a multiple of the identity, and $\kappa(TA)$ is the
ratio of its eigenvalues.

Two cautions. LOBPCG does not take this plain step: Rayleigh–Ritz picks the step length
and mixes in the previous direction, so the scale of $T$ never matters there. And in
two dimensions there is only one unwanted direction, which a well-scaled step can remove
on its own. The playground shows what $T\mathbf{r}$ looks like; the comparison further
down shows which $T$ wins when there are thousands of eigenvalues.

</div>

<VectorPlayground stage="preconditioned" />

<div className="narrow">

- **None, $T = I$.** The step $\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{r}$ multiplies the component along
  $\mathbf{v}_i$ by $1 + \rho - \lambda_i$, so it depends on the absolute size of the
  eigenvalues. For this matrix the $\mathbf{v}_2$ part flips sign and grows, so the
  plain step moves away from $\mathbf{v}_1$ and then stalls.
- **Diagonal, $T = \operatorname{diag}(A)^{-1}$.** Inverts only the diagonal entries.
  At an eigenvector angle of $0^\circ$ the matrix is diagonal and this is the exact
  inverse; at $45^\circ$ both diagonal entries are equal and $T$ is just a scaled
  identity. In two dimensions a well-scaled identity can still remove the single
  unwanted direction and even beat $A^{-1}$ in a plain step. With eigenvalues spread over
  $\kappa \propto N^2$, no single scale fits them all: $T$ must be cheap *in the basis
  where $A$ is nearly diagonal*.
- **Exact inverse, $T = A^{-1}$.** The step becomes
  $\mathbf{x} - A^{-1}(A\mathbf{x} - \rho\mathbf{x}) = \rho A^{-1}\mathbf{x}$, one step
  of inverse iteration: the tangent of the angle to $\mathbf{v}_1$ shrinks by the
  factor $\lambda_1/\lambda_2$.
- **Shifted inverse, $T = (A - \rho I)^{-1}$.** Inverting the shifted operator exactly is
  useless: $T\mathbf{r} = (A - \rho I)^{-1}(A - \rho I)\,\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{x}$. The
  proposed correction is $\mathbf{x}$ itself, it adds no new direction, and the step
  removes $\mathbf{x}$ entirely. $TA$ is also indefinite here, so its shape says nothing.

## Why must it be cheap?

$T$ is applied in every iteration, to every band, at every wavevector. A diagram with
12 bands, 61 wavevectors and about 20 iterations per wavevector applies it some
15,000 times, and a parameter study repeats that for every crystal.

How expensive an exact inverse is depends on where the material sits. In TM, Blaze's
operator is $A = |\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2$, diagonal in Fourier space, and the
permittivity enters only through the mass operator $B$. Its exact inverse costs two
fast Fourier transforms, and Blaze applies essentially that.

In TE, the permittivity sits between the two derivatives, $A = -\nabla\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla$.
$\varepsilon^{-1}$ couples every plane wave to every other, so $A$ is a dense matrix in
both bases, even though Blaze applies it with FFTs. On a $128 \times 128$ grid,
$N = 16{,}384$ unknowns: forming $A^{-1}$ takes a dense factorization of about
$N^3/3 \approx 1.5\times10^{12}$ operations, and its $N^2 \approx 2.7\times10^{8}$
complex entries need about 4 GB, at every wavevector, because $A$ changes with
$\mathbf{k}$. Each application then costs another $2N^2 \approx 5\times10^{8}$
operations. A fast Fourier transform of the same field takes on the order of
$5N\log_2 N \approx 10^{6}$. Solving $A\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{r}$ exactly would cost far
more than the eigenproblem the preconditioner is meant to speed up.

Construction has to be cheap as well. Because $A$ depends on $\mathbf{k}$, $T$ is
rebuilt at every wavevector; Blaze's preconditioners need only the values
$|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2$ and the permittivity grid, which cost $O(N)$ to set up.

## Why is an approximation good enough?

Three reasons:

1. **Inside LOBPCG, Rayleigh–Ritz takes care of scale.** $T$ only has to supply useful
   directions. Multiplying $T$ by a constant changes nothing at all: the trial subspace
   stays the same. (The plain step in the playground has no such help, which is why its
   *None* option depends on the size of $A$.)
2. **What matters is how evenly $T$ rebalances the spectrum,** roughly the condition
   number of $TA$, not how closely $T$ matches $A^{-1}$ entry by entry. A $T$ that
   captures how the eigenvalues grow with $|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2$ removes the
   dependence on grid resolution, even if it treats the material only approximately.
3. **Exactness can even hurt.** The exact inverse of the shifted operator returns
   $\mathbf{x}$ itself. A dense exact inverse of $A$ needs fewer iterations, but each one
   costs so much more that it loses on total work, as the comparison below shows.

The comparison below runs single-vector LOBPCG on a one-dimensional photonic crystal
with the structure of Blaze's TE problem, $A = -\tfrac{d}{dx}\,\varepsilon^{-1}\tfrac{d}{dx}$,
discretized on $N$ grid points. A layer of permittivity $\varepsilon$ fills 5/16 of the
cell, and the wavevector sits at the edge of the Brillouin zone. The Fourier diagonal
inverts the same operator for a uniform medium with the average
$\langle\varepsilon^{-1}\rangle$: one division per plane wave, the form of Blaze's TM
preconditioner. (MPB's transverse projection, Blaze's TE choice, would even be exact on
this model, because in one dimension every field is a gradient; in two dimensions it is
an approximation.) The exact inverse is priced as a dense factorization, as it would be
for Blaze's plane-wave TE operator. All four start from the same field and stop at a
relative residual of $10^{-7}$.

</div>

<div className="narrow">

Without a preconditioner, the iteration count doubles each time the grid doubles:
LOBPCG needs roughly $\sqrt{\kappa}$ iterations, and $\sqrt{\kappa}$ grows like $N$. The
diagonal of $A$ does not help. It rescales each grid point by its local stiffness, the
same for smooth and oscillating fields, so it cannot see how the eigenvalues grow; at
$\varepsilon = 13$ it is even slower than no preconditioner. The Fourier diagonal needs
14 to 17 iterations at every resolution. The exact inverse needs only 6 or 7, but its
factorization makes it 40 to 50 times more expensive than the Fourier diagonal at
$N = 512$, and since the factorization grows like $N^3$, the gap widens about fourfold
with each doubling of the grid. For $\varepsilon = 1$ the medium is uniform: the
Fourier diagonal *is* the exact inverse, both take 5 iterations, and the diagonal of
$A$ coincides with no preconditioner.

## How does Blaze precondition Maxwell’s equations?

Blaze stores fields on a real-space grid and switches to plane waves with fast
Fourier transforms. Derivatives become multiplications by $i(\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G})$,
so the derivative part of the operator is diagonal in Fourier space: it multiplies
each plane wave by $|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2$. The permittivity is diagonal in real
space. The stiff part, the one that grows with resolution, lives in Fourier space; the
material, which varies on the scale of the crystal, lives in real space. Blaze's
preconditioners invert the first exactly and approximate the second.

</div>

<BlazePipelines />

<div className="narrow">

- **TM.** The operator is $A = |\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2$ in Fourier space, and the
  material sits in the mass operator $B = \varepsilon$. Blaze applies
  $T = 1\,/\,\bigl(|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2/\langle\varepsilon\rangle + \sigma\bigr)$,
  plus a small constant floor: one FFT, one division per plane wave, one inverse FFT.
  The mean permittivity $\langle\varepsilon\rangle$ places the shift relative to the
  eigenvalues $\lambda \approx |\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2/\langle\varepsilon\rangle$
  of the generalized problem.
- **At $\Gamma$,** the plane wave with $\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G} = \mathbf{0}$ is the
  constant zero-frequency field, which the solver deflates; Blaze sets $T$ to zero on
  it. The shift $\sigma$ is half the smallest nonzero $|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2$ at
  each wavevector, which keeps $T$ balanced for the longest waves near $\Gamma$.
- **TE.** The permittivity sits inside the operator, $A = -\nabla\cdot\varepsilon^{-1}\nabla$.
  In band-structure runs Blaze uses MPB's transverse-projection
  preconditioner:<Citation id={2} /> it undoes each derivative in Fourier space and
  multiplies by $\varepsilon$ on the grid, inverting the $\varepsilon^{-1}$ in the
  middle. That takes six FFTs per application and never forms a matrix. For a uniform
  medium it inverts $A$ apart from the shift; where $\varepsilon$ varies it is an
  approximation.

Both cost $O(N\log N)$ per application and $O(N)$ to set up at each wavevector. The
roadmap continues from here: a [learned preconditioner](/roadmap#learned-preconditioner)
keeps these FFT kernels and learns positive spectral weights per wavevector, and
[self-tuning solves](/roadmap#self-tuning-solves) choose the shift from the cost of
recent wavevectors.

## Summary

</div>

<div className="pc-summary">
<div>

**The residual says what is wrong.** $\mathbf{r} = A\mathbf{x} - \rho\mathbf{x}$
vanishes only for eigenvectors, but weights each error component by $\lambda_i - \rho$.

</div>
<div>

**The preconditioner says what to do about it.** $T \approx A^{-1}$ turns those
weights into $1 - \rho/\lambda_i$, and the residual becomes a correction.

</div>
<div>

**It must be cheap.** Applied thousands of times per band diagram and rebuilt at every
wavevector, it is made of FFTs and divisions, never dense matrices.

</div>
<div>

**Approximate is enough; exact can be worse.** Rayleigh–Ritz fixes the scale,
capturing the growth of $|\mathbf{k}+\mathbf{G}|^2$ removes the grid dependence, and
the exact shifted inverse adds nothing.

</div>
