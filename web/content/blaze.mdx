# Blaze2D: Numerical Methods and Benchmarks

*Historical measurements of band calculations, memory use, and numerical agreement.*

<a href={getAssetPath('/reports/blaze2d-technical-report.pdf')} target="_blank" rel="noreferrer">Download the PDF ↗</a>

</div>


<div className="narrow">

Photonic band structure calculations commonly rely on the Plane Wave Expansion (PWE) method, and [MIT Photonic Bands (MPB)](https://mpb.readthedocs.io/en/latest/) has long served as a widely used reference implementation for it.<Citation id={1} /><Citation id={2} /> MPB is trusted for its accuracy and is therefore a natural baseline for performance comparisons.

Blaze is a Rust solver using LOBPCG and mixed-precision arithmetic.<Citation id={3} /><Citation id={4} /> This report presents historical measurements of band calculations. It separates observed timings and memory use from hypotheses about their causes.

These historical benchmarks use square and hexagonal lattice configurations from [Joannopoulos and colleagues' 1997 paper](https://www.nature.com/articles/386143a0).<Citation id={5} /> Unless otherwise specified, all data reflects the square lattice configuration at a resolution of 64, computing the lowest 8 bands with 20 k-points per segment between high-symmetry points. Series 4 and 5 record MPB tolerance `1e-7` and Blaze tolerance `1e-4`. The archived web datasets do not consistently record hardware identifiers or solver revisions, which limits reproducibility and cross-release comparisons.

## Single-Core Performance

The computational cost of PWE solvers is dominated by Fast Fourier Transforms (FFTs).<Citation id={1} /> In this plane-wave formulation, Transverse Electric (TE) modes are more expensive to solve than Transverse Magnetic (TM) modes, because each operator application requires six FFTs rather than two for TM.

In these recorded single-core workloads, the f64 and mixed-precision Blaze runs are faster than the MPB runs. The mixed-precision measurements give an average speed ratio of about ``2.9×``. This ratio depends on the geometry, numerical settings, and execution environment.
</div>

## Multi-Core Performance

The benchmark compares MPB threading within individual solves with Blaze scheduling independent configurations.<Citation id={6} /><Citation id={7} /> The cost of synchronization and data movement can limit scaling for small problems.

Blaze avoids this overhead by parallelizing entire jobs rather than individual operations, a strategy optimized for large-scale parameter sweeps.

The recorded 16-thread runs give an average speed ratio of ``11.9×``. Blaze f64 times are approximately ``240–350 ms``, while mixed-precision times are about ``160 ms``. These timings alone do not identify a unique hardware bottleneck.
</div>

## Memory Efficiency

The recorded process-memory measurements are approximately ``190 MB`` for MPB over much of this sweep. Process memory includes runtime and library allocations, so this observation does not establish that MPB reserves a fixed solver workspace.<Citation id={7} />

Blaze uses less measured peak memory in these runs. A smaller working set can reduce data movement, but cache residency requires direct profiling and cannot be inferred from the memory ratio alone.

For a square grid with side length $N$, field storage grows as $N^2$ per band. Eigensolver workspaces also depend on band count, precision, and retained outputs. Process-level measurements may conceal that growth over a limited range.

</div>

## Accuracy Validation

The reduction in precision and memory footprint raises a direct question: do we gain speed at the cost accuracy? To answer this question, we compared the eigenfrequencies from Blaze against a high-precision MPB reference along the full $\Gamma \to X \to M \to \Gamma$ path. In the plots below, the MPB bands are drawn as lines and the Blaze eigenvalues as markers.

The comparison computes 20 bands and displays the lowest 10. Frequencies are compared as sorted eigenvalue sets at each wavevector, avoiding sensitivity to band labels near crossings and the upper edge of the solved window. This historical plotting convention differs from the current API, which preserves the tracked path returned by the solver.

## Deviation Analysis

The recorded median relative frequency difference from MPB is about $1 \times 10^{-4}$. The maximum is approximately $4 \times 10^{-4}$ for TM and $3 \times 10^{-4}$ for TE. These discrepancies measure agreement between runs; they are not certified bounds on discretization error.

The f32 and f64 Blaze runs differ by a median of about $1 \times 10^{-7}$ and a maximum below $5 \times 10^{-5}$. Storage precision therefore has a smaller effect than the observed MPB difference in this dataset. Prior mixed-precision eigensolver studies provide related context.<Citation id={4} />

</div>

<div className="narrow">
## Parallel Scaling

The two solvers parallelize in different ways. MPB splits the work inside a single solve across threads, while Blaze runs many independent solves at the same time, one per thread, which suits the parameter sweeps it is built for. The charts sweep the thread count from ``1`` to ``16`` and measure throughput in solves per second, for a small problem ($N = 16$) and a large one ($N = 128$).

For the recorded $N=16$ problem, Blaze throughput increases from about ``28`` to ``220`` solves per second across 16 threads. MPB threaded throughput stays near ``14`` solves per second. Separate MPB processes scale better than its threaded mode in this experiment.

For $N=128$, Blaze throughput increases by about ``3×`` across the thread-count sweep. This supports scheduling independent configurations for these workloads; it does not establish a general limit on MPB parallelism.
</div>

## Scaling with Resolution

Resolution sets the overall cost: the number of plane waves grows as $N^2$ and the FFTs as $N^2 \log N$. The bar chart sweeps the grid from $N = 16$ to $N = 192$ and compares wall-clock time against MPB.

Blaze is faster at each sampled resolution in this dataset. At $N=192$, the recorded times are approximately ``8.0 s`` versus ``39 s`` for TM and ``12 s`` versus ``70 s`` for TE. MPB timings vary non-monotonically with some grid sizes. FFT factorization and planning are possible contributors, requiring separate profiling.

The log-log plot compares empirical trends over the sampled resolutions. Its fitted slopes should not be extrapolated beyond this range without further measurements.
</div>

## Convergence and Iteration Count

Beyond the cost of the *entire* single iteration, the number of iterations needed to converge each $k$-point matters.

Mean iteration counts are approximately ``4.5`` for Blaze TM and ``5.6`` for Blaze TE, compared with ``7.5`` and ``11.9`` for MPB. The recorded tolerances are ``1e-4`` for Blaze and ``1e-7`` for MPB, so these are not equal-tolerance iteration comparisons. Both implementations use eigenvalue changes in their stopping criteria. [MPB documents this convention explicitly](https://mpb.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Python_User_Interface/). Fresh residuals must be checked separately.

Maximum recorded iteration counts are ``39`` for MPB TM and ``85`` for MPB TE, compared with ``12`` and ``24`` for Blaze. Their locations vary along the path. The measurements do not isolate whether preconditioning, subspace updates, or stopping settings cause the difference.
</div>

## Conclusion

The recorded experiments show lower Blaze wall-clock times and peak process memory for the tested configurations. The reported ratios are specific to those measurements and their numerical settings. A matched comparison of release 0.7 requires new runs with explicit solver revisions, hardware, retained outputs, and residual checks.

Blaze schedules independent configurations and uses mixed-precision storage with f64 critical reductions. These design choices support repeated crystal calculations and scientific data extraction.

Blaze exports projected operator and derivative data, including velocity matrices, effective masses, and formulation-specific geometric terms.<Citation id={9} /><Citation id={10} /><Citation id={11} /> MPB also exposes fields, eigenvectors, and group velocities. Blaze integrates parameter studies and projected extraction in one configuration and result contract.
</div>
