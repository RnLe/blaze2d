# Introduction

Blaze2D computes **photonic bands** and projected Maxwell operators for two-dimensional periodic dielectric structures. It is written in Rust and available through Python, `TOML` files, a native command, and the browser [Workbench](/workbench).

This page builds the physical picture those calculations rest on: what makes a crystal photonic, how a repeating structure is described, and what a band structure actually reports.

<IntroductionQuestions />

## What makes a crystal photonic?

A photonic crystal is a material whose **dielectric permittivity repeats in space**.
Alternating glass and air layers are a simple example. Light scattered by the repeated
interfaces interferes, allowing some waves to travel through the structure while
excluding others. The pattern and dielectric contrast determine that response.

</div>

<div className="narrow">

To describe a repeating pattern, separate its **lattice** from its **basis**. The lattice
specifies the translations: integer steps along two primitive vectors,
$\mathbf{a}_1$ and $\mathbf{a}_2$. The basis is the arrangement of objects repeated
at each lattice point. There are five Bravais lattice types in two dimensions.

</div>

<BravaisLattices />

<div className="narrow">

Place a dielectric rod at every point, or an air hole in a uniform dielectric, and
the lattice becomes a photonic crystal. A basis can also contain several objects.
For example, a honeycomb pattern uses a triangular lattice with two sites per cell.
The lattice sets the translations; the basis, object sizes, and materials set the
permittivity within each cell.

## Why does light form bands?

The geometry enters Maxwell's equations through the relative permittivity
$\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})$. For a lossless, nonmagnetic material without frequency
dispersion, the magnetic field obeys the **photonic master equation**:

<div className="intro-equation">

$$
\nabla \times \left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})}\nabla \times \mathbf{H}\right)
= \frac{\omega^2}{c^2}\mathbf{H}.
$$

</div>

Here $\mathbf{H}$ is a mode's magnetic field, $\omega$ its angular frequency, and $c$
the speed of light in vacuum. This is an **eigenvalue problem**: find the field patterns
whose shape is unchanged by the operator on the left. The scale factor is the
eigenvalue $\omega^2/c^2$.

If you know electronic band theory, the connection is direct. A periodic potential
acts on an electron's wavefunction in the Schrödinger equation. A periodic
permittivity acts on an electromagnetic field in Maxwell's equations. Both admit
Bloch waves and bands, although the fields, operators, and physical observables differ.

</div>

<IntroductionFigure
  src="/figures/intro/electronic-photonic-crystals.png"
  alt="Comparison slide: graphene with two carbon sites per cell and the Schrödinger equation on the left; a honeycomb arrangement of air holes in dielectric and the photonic master equation on the right."
  width={1565} height={801}
  caption="The same lattice language describes both systems. The electronic model resolves a wavefunction in an atomic potential; the photonic model describes the material through its macroscopic permittivity."
/>

<div className="narrow">

Periodicity lets us write each mode as a plane wave multiplied by a function that
repeats in every cell:

$$
\mathbf{H}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})
= e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\mathbf{u}_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x}).
$$

The **Bloch wavevector** $\mathbf{k}$ sets the phase change from cell to cell.
For each $\mathbf{k}$, the master equation gives a sequence of allowed frequencies.
Following the $n$th frequency as $\mathbf{k}$ changes gives a band,
$\omega_n(\mathbf{k})$.

## How do I read a band diagram?

A band diagram plots those frequencies along a path through the **Brillouin zone**,
the region of distinct Bloch wavevectors. Labels such as $\Gamma$, $X$, $M$, and $K$
mark points of lattice symmetry. The horizontal axis is a path in wavevector space,
not a position inside the crystal.

</div>

<IntroductionFigure
  src="/figures/intro/band-structure.svg"
  alt="Photonic bands for square dielectric rods on the left and triangular air holes on the right. Blue curves are TM and orange curves are TE. Shading marks a TM gap on the left and a shared TE and TM gap on the right."
  width={982.383125} height={321.03625}
  caption="Square rods in air (left) and a triangular lattice of air holes (right). Insets show the structures and the paths through their Brillouin zones."
/>

<div className="narrow">

Each curve is one allowed mode frequency. The vertical axis uses
$\omega a/(2\pi c)=a/\lambda_0$, where $a$ is the lattice constant and $\lambda_0$
the vacuum wavelength. This normalization makes the diagram independent of the
overall scale of a nondispersive crystal.

The blue curves are **TM** modes, with the electric field along the invariant
direction. Orange curves are **TE** modes, with the magnetic field in that direction.
On the left, the shaded interval excludes TM modes but still contains TE modes.
On the right, the shaded interval is shared by both polarizations. A complete
band gap requires an absence of modes throughout the Brillouin zone, so checking
only the plotted path is not sufficient to establish it.

Band slopes also carry information: $\nabla_{\mathbf{k}}\omega_n$ gives the group
velocity. A flatter band means slower propagation in the corresponding direction.
These frequencies and field patterns are the basic results returned by a band solver.

## How does Blaze solve the equation?

[MPB](https://mpb.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Developer_Information/) and Blaze use a **plane-wave expansion**
(PWE). Once the Bloch phase is separated out, the remaining field is periodic.
It can therefore be represented by a finite set of Fourier components, labelled
by reciprocal lattice vectors $\mathbf{G}$:

<div className="intro-equation">

$$
\begin{aligned}
u_{n\mathbf{k}}(\mathbf{x})
&\approx \sum_{\mathbf{G}} c_{n\mathbf{G}}(\mathbf{k})\,e^{i\mathbf{G}\cdot\mathbf{x}},\\[0.6em]
A(\mathbf{k})\,\mathbf{c}_n
&= \frac{\omega_n^2(\mathbf{k})}{c^2}\,B\,\mathbf{c}_n.
\end{aligned}
$$

</div>

For Blaze's 2D scalar problems, $u$ is the periodic part of $H_z$ for TE or $E_z$ for TM.
The coefficients $\mathbf{c}_n$ are the unknowns. $A$ represents the differential
operator; $B$ is the identity for TE and the dielectric weight for TM.
An iterative eigensolver finds the requested bands at each wavevector. Increasing
the grid resolution adds Fourier components, and convergence checks show whether
the chosen resolution is sufficient.

<ol className="intro-solver-steps" aria-label="Band calculation steps">
  <li><span>01</span><strong>Define the cell</strong><small>Lattice, objects, permittivity</small></li>
  <li><span>02</span><strong>Solve at each k</strong><small>Periodic fields and frequencies</small></li>
  <li><span>03</span><strong>Trace the bands</strong><small>Dispersion, gaps, mode profiles</small></li>
</ol>

Blaze currently supports two-dimensional circles and positive scalar dielectric
materials. This covers rods, holes, and multi-object bases on any 2D Bravais lattice.
Its rods and holes extend uniformly along the third direction.

## Why calculate many different crystals?

One band diagram answers a question about one configuration. Designing a crystal
or building a model often requires a **parameter study**: repeat the calculation
while changing the geometry or material.

<div className="intro-parameter-cards">
  <div><strong>Rod or hole radius</strong><span>Where does a gap open or close?</span></div>
  <div><strong>Positions in the basis</strong><span>How do alignment and symmetry change the bands?</span></div>
  <div><strong>Dielectric constant</strong><span>How does material contrast shift the frequencies?</span></div>
</div>

In many short solves, geometry setup can become a substantial repeated cost.
MPB's [geometry initialization](https://mpb.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Developer_Information/#dielectric-function-computation)
samples and averages the dielectric near interfaces.
Blaze targets this overhead with specialized circle geometry and analytic boundary
area fractions, and schedules independent configurations across CPU cores. Its
ordered sweeps retain the parameter values alongside each result.

The [technical report](/blaze) compares complete calculation times against MPB
under stated settings. Those timings include both setup and solving; the benefit
depends on the geometry, resolution, and number of requested bands.

## What can operator data tell us?

A band diagram shows where the modes lie. It does not, by itself, tell us how two
modes couple when a structure changes. That requires matrix elements of the
operator and its derivatives between the computed fields.

MPB already exposes frequencies, electromagnetic fields, raw eigenvectors, and
group velocities through its [Python interface](https://mpb.readthedocs.io/en/latest/Python_User_Interface/).
It does not provide Blaze's prepared collection of projected derivative matrices
and associated coordinate and phase-convention metadata. Deriving these from MPB
output requires an additional extraction workflow.

Both solvers use **matrix-free operator application**: they evaluate $A\mathbf{v}$
for a trial field without storing the full matrix $A$. Fourier transforms connect
spatial derivatives to material multiplication. This saves memory, and it still
allows small projected matrices to be computed by applying an operator to selected
fields and taking their inner products.

For example, let $L$ be the periodic TE operator. A parameter $p$ such as a wavevector
component or an object's displacement gives the derivative matrix

<div className="intro-equation">

$$
\begin{aligned}
D^{(p)}_{mn}
&= \left\langle u_m,\frac{\partial L}{\partial p}\,u_n\right\rangle,\\[0.6em]
L u_n&=\lambda_n u_n,\qquad \lambda_n=\frac{\omega_n^2}{c^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

</div>

Here the fields are normalized within the unit cell. Away from degeneracies,
diagonal entries describe the first-order change in an eigenvalue; off-diagonal
entries describe mixing between different modes. Near a degeneracy, the coupled
bands must be treated together.

| Data available in Blaze | Physical use |
| --- | --- |
| Wavevector derivatives and curvature matrices | Propagation, coupling between bands, and effective masses understood as band curvature |
| Derivatives with respect to object displacement | Perturbations caused by changing the relative alignment of objects in a cell |
| Cross-band matrix elements and overlaps between sampled states | Reduced models that retain selected bands and track changes in their basis |

For TM, the dielectric weight and its derivatives must also be included; Blaze
records those separately. The [operator reference](/configuration#operators-registry-studies-and-stencils)
specifies the available quantities and conventions.

This is the connection to the [master's thesis](/thesis). A twisted photonic crystal
can be described through many locally periodic crystals whose alignment changes
slowly in space. Their bands supply the local frequencies. Their projected operator
data supply coefficients for an effective model of the slow variation. Blaze was
built to make both parts of that calculation practical.

To calculate a first band diagram, open an [example](/workbench?view=examples) or follow the
[installation guide](/installation).
